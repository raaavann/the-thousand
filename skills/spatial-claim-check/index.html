A data-visualisation skill DE705 spatial-claim-check

Does position mean anything?

In a scatterplot, position is a measurement. In a force layout, a t-SNE or a bubble pack, position is an output — and the viewer cannot tell the difference. This skill audits the spatial claim a chart is making, and refuses to let it ship unmeasured. Below, it is run on a genre constellation of 1,000 films — including on the rebuild, where it found a regression its own author had missed.

01

The problem

A chart can lie with coordinates and never be caught

Every chart a reader has ever seen taught them that near means alike. Computed-position charts inherit that trust without earning it.

A force-directed graph, an embedding, a packed bubble chart and a word cloud all produce an x and a y for every mark. None of those numbers came off an axis. They are the output of a solver that was optimising for something — edge tension, neighbourhood preservation, packing efficiency — which may or may not be the thing the caption claims.

The failure is quiet. Nothing looks broken. The clusters are crisp, the shape is organic, the picture is frequently beautiful. It is simply not about what it appears to be about — most often because the layout has rediscovered which category is most common rather than which things belong together.

So the skill starts by refusing to discuss aesthetics at all. It asks for one sentence first.

Two marks near each other in this chart means ———.

If the sentence cannot be finished, position is decoration, and the honest move is to replace the layout with one that encodes something — a sorted grid, a small multiple, a ranked list — not to tune it. If it can be finished, it can be measured, and everything after this point is arithmetic.

02

The skill

Seven checks and one number

Checks run in order; check 1 is a gate. The eighth item is not a check but a habit, and it is the one that does the real work.

  1. State the claimComplete the sentence above. If it cannot be completed, stop — do not optimise a claim that does not exist.
  2. Frequency dominationIf one category exceeds ~40% of rows, any raw-count layout collapses toward it. It becomes the nearest neighbour of nearly everything for no reason but being common.
  3. What the method supportsPer method, name the readings it forbids. t-SNE cluster sizes and between-cluster distances are meaningless. Bubble-pack adjacency is a packing artifact. Sankey node order is a crossing heuristic, not a ranking.
  4. DeterminismRe-run it. If the picture changes, it is not a map — a cluster cannot be pointed at twice. Seed every stochastic step.
  5. Aggregation collapseWhen marks sit at a centroid of anchors: does the common member dominate the mean, and does cluster radius exceed the distance to the nearest foreign anchor?
  6. Legibility at NLayout area ÷ mark count. Can the mark carry its encoding at that size? A dot carries a value in radius and lightness at 3px; an image carries nothing.
  7. Caveat and coordinatesOne sentence in the frame saying what position does and does not mean — and a way to hand the coordinates over, so the claim can be checked rather than trusted.

Then: measure it, don't argue about it

Layout-quality arguments are unfalsifiable until someone writes down a number. Every claim shape has a corresponding scale-free test, and every test has a random baseline that must be reported beside it — 40% sounds poor until you know that shuffling scores 12%.

# claim: "items sit among the groups they belong to"
score = share of items whose nearest anchor is one of their own groups
base  = mean groups per item ÷ number of anchors

# claim: "similar items are near each other"
score = k-NN label agreement — share of k nearest neighbours sharing a label
base  = overall label prevalence

And ablate. Changing four things at once and reporting one improvement is not evidence. Vary one at a time and publish the table — including the rows where the change you were proudest of did nothing.

03

The audit

Run on “1,000 Films, 21 Genres”

The subject places 1,000 films by genre: Drama fixed at the centre of the field, the other twenty on a circle around it, each film at the unweighted mean of its genres' positions, relaxed with a force simulation. Marks are 22 × 32 px poster images. Its stated claim is a good one — “films with more than one genre sit between the clusters they belong to” — which is precisely what makes it auditable.

Verdict

The claim, measured

For every film, find the nearest of the 21 genre anchors and ask whether it is one of that film's own genres. No radius, no units, no dependence on canvas size. Random placement scores about 12%.

The original scores 55.4% overall — and the total is misleading. It aces the trivial case, where a single-genre film is placed exactly on its anchor and barely moves, then does worst on the case the piece exists to show. Three-genre films are 64.6% of the collection and the entire premise of “sitting between clusters”; they land nearest a genre they do not belong to more than half the time.

04

The mechanism

Raw counts mostly rediscover what is common

Dividing co-occurrence by the union of the two genres — Jaccard — corrects for how common each one is. The reordering is not subtle: every pair whose only distinction was containing Drama falls away, and pairs that are specifically each other's rise.

Geometrically, the consequence is a single number. Under raw counts Drama sits 0.039 from the centre of the layout while the average anchor sits at 0.302 — pinned at the gravitational centre, at 13% of a typical anchor's distance from it. Because 72.4% of films carry Drama into their centroid, most of the collection is dragged into an undifferentiated middle. Under Jaccard it moves out to 0.071.

05

Ablation

Four changes, varied one at a time

Reporting the four fixes as a bundle would prove nothing. Each was varied alone against the same metric. The shipped configuration is confirmed against the live artifact via window.__spatialAudit(), which returns the same four figures — so the offline reproduction is faithful and the rest of the table can be trusted.

Including the unflattering rows

Jaccard is the single biggest lever, +16.4 points. It earns its place.

The aspect-fill fit adds 2.1, not the transformation it appeared to be. Judged by eye it looked like the change that spread the layout out. Measured, it is a minor contributor — and the two measures produce nearly identical anchor adjacency, so the earlier claim that Jaccard “spread the layout” was wrong. What it changes is how far Drama sits from the pack, not who is beside whom.

The rarity-weighted centroid contributes zero points overall. It redistributes — two-genre films up 6.8, three-genre films down 2.8 — and it populates the outer clusters visually, which is why it was kept. Saying it improved the claim would be false.

The cluster radius cap adds 11.1, and exists only because the audit was also run on the rebuild.

The regression the skill caught

Before the cap, single-genre films scored 29.5% — far worse than the original build's 98.1%. The rebuild had quietly broken the easiest case while fixing the hard one. The Drama-only cluster was spreading 145 px while Comedy's anchor sat 108 px away, so its outer stars were literally nearer a genre they did not belong to.

The rule that fixes it: a cluster may grow to at most 0.46 × the distance from its centroid to the nearest anchor it does not belong to. Past the halfway point, a chart contradicts its own caption.

What a second pass found

The skill was run again on the redesigned page, and two checks that had passed on the original failed on the rebuild, both because the rebuild was more ambitious. Its genre labels were unreadable over the dense core and collided with each other at narrow widths; the original had never triggered that because its labels sat in empty space, which is to say it passed by accident. And the rebuild added depth and parallax, which is a spatial claim of its own: an axis the reader can see but that encodes nothing. Depth is now bound to rating, which it already needed to show.

One correction to the numbers on this page. The metric is scale-free, but the layout is recomputed per viewport, so the anchors move when the frame's aspect ratio changes and the score moves with them. The same configuration measured 69.3%, 69.2% and 68.5% across three container sizes. All three are right for the frame they were taken in; printing any of them to one decimal as though it were a property of the layout was not.

A third pass fixed the cause rather than the wording. The layout no longer re-solves per viewport: it is solved once and fitted, so the same arrangement survives every screen shape and holds the score to 64.5 / 64.1 / 64.3 across landscape, narrow landscape and portrait. That costs about five points of absolute accuracy, which is the trade for a map a reader can return to and find unchanged. An outside critique of that same build then found three things these checks had passed — rating riding on the weakest visual channels, an unstated 13.7× area exaggeration, and a twinkle modulating the very channel that encoded rating. All three are folded back into the skill.

Four of twenty-one genres — Biography, Mystery, Family and War — had zero films within 120 px of their own label in the original, while holding 109, 99, 56 and 51 films between them. The label said Mystery and not one of its ninety-nine films was anywhere near it.

07

Correction

What this audit got wrong first

An early pass recorded that the original rendered a near-empty field with almost no posters loading. That was wrong, and it is on the page because the way it went wrong is instructive.

The browser running the check was not painting. That suppressed loading="lazy" so completely that zero image requests were ever issued — the field looked empty because nothing had been asked for. Forcing a real paint showed 923 of 1,000 posters loading into a dense, rather striking mass.

The actual defect was never the loading. It was that a thousand unreadable marks packed into one blob say nothing about genre — a spatial-claim failure, and one only visible once the thing genuinely rendered. Hence the last line of the skill's Never list: never conclude from a rendering you have not actually seen.
